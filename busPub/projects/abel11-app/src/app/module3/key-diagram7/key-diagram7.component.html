Overview of the AD/AS model

  • The price level, , is on the vertical axis, and output, , is on the horizontal axis .
  • The aggregate demand (AD) curve shows the aggregate quantity of output demanded at each price level.
  • In the short run, the price level is fixed, and firms supply whatever level of output is demanded. Thus, the short-run aggregate supply (SRAS) curve is horizontal.
  • In the long run, firms produce the amount of output that maximizes their profits, which is the full-employment level of output.
  • So, the long-run aggregate-supply curve, LRAS, is vertical.
  • The economy is in general equilibrium at the intersection of all three curves.

Factors that shift the AD curve (part 1): IS curve shifters (saving)

  • The AD curve shifts up and to the right if
    • Wealth increases,
    • Expected future output increases,
    • Government purchases increase,
    • Taxes decrease.
  • To restore general equilibrium, the price level changes, causing the SRAS curve to shift.

Factors that shift the AD curve (part 2): IS curve shifters

  • The AD curve shifts up and to the right if
    • Expected future TFP increases,
    • The effective tax rate on capital decreases.
  • To restore general equilibrium, the price level changes, causing the SRAS curve to shift.

Factors that shift the AD curve (part 3): LM curve shfiters

  • The AD curve shifts up and to the right if
    • Nominal money supply increases,
    • Expected inflation increases,
    • Nominal interest rate on monetary assets decreases.
  • To restore general equilibrium, the price level changes, causing the SRAS curve to shift.

Factors that shift the SRAS curve

  • Any factor, such as an increase in the costs of production, that leads firms to increase prices in the short-run will shift the short-run aggregate supply (SRAS) curve up.
  • Conversely, a factor that decreases the costs of production will shift the SRAS curve down.
  • To restore general equilibrium, the price level changes, causing the SRAS curve to shift.

Factors that shift the LRAS curve

  • Any factor that increases the full-employment level of output shifts the long-run aggregate supply (LRAS) curve to the right
    • A beneficial supply shock that causes an increase in total factor productivity,
    • An increase in labor supply,
    • An increase in the capital stock.
  • To restore general equilibrium, the price level changes, causing the SRAS curve to shift.
