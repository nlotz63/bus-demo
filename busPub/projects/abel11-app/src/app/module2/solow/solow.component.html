The per-worker production function

  • The per-worker production function relates the amount of output produced per worker to the capital–labor ratio.
  • The per-worker production function slopes upward from left to right because an increase in the capital–labor ratio raises the amount of output produced per worker.
  • The bowed shape of the production function reflects the diminishing marginal productivity of capital.

Determining the capital-labor ratio in the steady state

  • The steady-state capital-labor ratio is determined by the condition that saving per worker, , equals steady-state investment per worker, .
  • The steady-state capital-labor ratio is where the saving curve and the steady-state investment line cross.

The effect of an increased saving rate on the steady-state capital-labor ratio

  • An increase in the saving rate shifts the saving curve upwards.
  • The point where saving per worker equals steady-state investment per worker corresponds to a higher capital-labor ratio.
  • Thus, a higher saving rate raises the steady-state capital-labor ratio.

The effect of a higher population growth rate on the steady-state capital-labor ratio

  • An increase in the population growth rate increases steady-state investment per worker, causing the steady-state investment curve to pivot up and to the left, with a higher slope.
  • The point where saving per worker equals steady-state investment per worker corresponds to a lower capital-labor ratio.
  • Thus, a higher population growth rate reduces the steady-state capital-labor ratio.

The effect of a productivity improvement on the steady-state capital-labor ratio

  • A productivity improvement shifts the production function and the saving curve upwards.
  • The point where saving per worker equals steady-state investment per worker corresponds to a higher capital-labor ratio.
  • Thus, a higher saving rate raises the steady-state capital-labor ratio.
